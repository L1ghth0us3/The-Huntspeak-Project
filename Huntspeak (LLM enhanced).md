## Grammar

### Syntax

#### Sentence Structure

SVO (Subject, Verb (adverb), (adjective) Object(s))  
When an irregular verb is used then the adjective comes before the verb-object it refers to.  
Vocal stress always rests on the first syllable

#### Subject Pronouns

Example: to write \- /v-d-b/

* ɪ vɪdab \- I write  
* Su vudab \- You write  
* Se vedab \- He/She/It writes  
* Tɪ vɪdab \- We write  
* Tu vudab \- You (pl.) write  
* Te vedab \- They write

###  Morphology

#### Verbs

##### Tenses:

	There are 5 tenses in Huntspeak. Present, Past recent, Past long, Future and  
	Hypothetical.  
	While Present and Past are self-explanatory, Future recent describes things not older than  
	a week. Future long describes anything beyond that.  
The hypothetical auxiliary “iqe” is always used in conjunction with another tense and always placed directly in front of the verb and indicates a hypothetical scenario.

	Example: to fight \- /r-s-ʃ/ & many/a lot \- /dæl/

* ɪ dæl rɪsaʃ \- I fight a lot  
* ɪ dæl rɪseʃ \- I fought a lot  
* ɪ dæl rɪsʌʃ \- I will fight a lot  
* ɪ dæl ike rɪseʃ \- If I fought a lot


  

  The Imperfective relates to an action that is or was ongoing for a timeframe, while the perfect is always used for a single timeframe.


* Tu dæl ike rɪsʌʃ   \- If I will fight a lot  
* Tu dæl ike rɪsʌaʃ \- If I will be fighting a lot  
  		  
  	

|  | Perfective | Imperfective |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Present** | \-a- | \-aa- |
| **Past Recent** | \-e- | \-ea- |
| **Past Long** | \-u- | \-ua- |
| **Future** | \-â-, \-ʌ- | \-âa-, \-ʌa- |
| **Hypothetical** | Iqe, ike (auxiliary) | \- |


  

  ##### Triconsonantal Roots:

  Nearly all of the Huntspeak verbs are made with a sequence of three consonantal roots. The placement of other consonants and vowels determines the conjugation of the word.


  Example: to write \- /v-d-b/


* ɪ vɪdab \- I write/I am writing  
* ɪ vɪdeb \- I wrote  
* ɪ vɪdʌb \- I will write/I am going to write

  Typically, the first vowel mimics the vowel sound of the subject, while the second vowel indicates if it is past, present or future tense, with /a/ being present, /e/ being past and /ʌ/ being future tenses.


  In the case of only two root consonants, the second vowel dictating the tense will end the world.


  Example: to eat \- /l-b- \-/


* ɪ lɪba \- I eat/I am eating  
* ɪ lɪbe \- I ate  
* ɪ lɪbʌ \- I will eat/I am going to eat


  ##### Irregular Verbs:

  	Some basic verbs (to be, to have, to do, to hunt, etc) are irregular.  
  	These verbs are used as prefixes to the object and leave out the subject identifying vowel.  
  	They still contain a vowel to indicate the tense.  
    
  	Example: to have \- /v-/  
  	  
* Tɪ vajʌl \- We have a meeting  
* Tɪ vejʌl \- We had a meeting  
* Tɪ vʌjʌl \- We will have a meeting

  ##### Multiple Verbs: 

  	More complex sentences will change the structure of it slightly.

  	Example:  
  		

* ɪ danenad tye \- I cause the animal to move  
  * Here, the verb prefix “da-” refers to the subject “I” while the verb “nenad” refers to the object. The pronoun “S**e**” (It) is implied in “n**e**nad”  
* ɪ mɪvat tu tye danenad \- I trust you cause the animal to move  
  * In this example we have a sentence structure of SVOOVV.   
    The verb will be put at the end and the order of the verbs and subjects stay the same


  	Auxiliary words are noted with the suffix “-ba”.  
    	  
    	Example:

  	

* Î qîrad-ba lîba qäru \- I want to eat food.  
  * The auxiliary verb “want to” (“q-r-d”) supports the main verb “eat” (“l-b-”)

  			Which means, “want to” will have “-ba” added at the end.

#### Nouns:

##### Plurals:

	There is no Plural form of nouns. Plurality is defined by either counting the amount or using  
words like “many, few, all”  
	If there are two of the same vowel following after each other because of this, there will be a  
	Phonological pause

	Example:

		You see my (two) ears \- “Su vusat amî bi-idwa \- \[bi.idʋa\]  
I saw many animals in the forest \- “Î vîset däl tye yat wäwi”

#### Adjectives:

	

##### 	Comparatives and Superlatives:

		Letting an Adjective compare with something else, is done via 2 different suffixes.  
		\-du is used for comparatives  
		\-vu is used for superlatives

		Example:

* Su amî **qaar-vu** sha-shidyävu \- You are my **best** friend  
* Kharau hâiru sha-**sil-du** tiau nätum \- This cave is **darker** than the Night  
* Se nenad **shai-du** tiau tye \- She moves **faster** than an animal

##### 	Noun-to-Adjective conversion:

To create an adjective from a noun, take the noun and add the suffix “-si” on the end.

Example:

* Death → Deadly \= ikhel → ikhel-si  
  		

			

## **Clause Linking**

Huntspeak distinguishes between **coordination**, **event chaining**, and **subordination**.

* Coordination joins elements of equal grammatical importance.  
* Event chaining presents a connected sequence of events.  
* Subordination marks one clause as the cause, condition, purpose, time frame, or background of another clause.

Unless otherwise stated, every linked clause remains a complete finite clause. It retains its own subject and conjugated predicate.

---

### **Coordination**

Huntspeak uses three basic coordinators:

* **wa** — and  
* **ara** — but  
* **ura** — or

A coordinator may join nouns, phrases, predicates, or complete clauses.

#### **Shared subjects**

When coordinated predicates share the same subject, the subject does not need to be repeated.

*Tî sîluav wa qîhuap shilaqar.*  
“We survived and helped the tribe.”

Each predicate remains fully conjugated.

#### **Changed subjects**

When the subject changes, the new subject is stated before its predicate.

**Subject₁ \+ Predicate₁ \+ coordinator \+ Subject₂ \+ Predicate₂**

---

### **Event Chaining**

Closely connected events may be placed beside one another without **wa**.

**Subject \+ Predicate₁, Predicate₂, Predicate₃**

The events are normally understood to:

1. belong to the same episode;  
2. occur in the order stated;  
3. continue with the same subject unless a new subject is introduced.

*Î vîsat tye, î rîsaš.*  
“I see the animal, then I fight.”

The repeated pronoun may be omitted when the subject clearly remains the same:

*Î vîsat tye, rîsaš.*  
“I see the animal and fight.”

Event chaining indicates sequence or close narrative connection. It does not inherently mean “because,” “although,” “if,” or “therefore.”

---

## **Cause: naru**

**Naru** introduces the reason or cause of an event. It corresponds to “because” or causal “since.”

### **Main clause first**

**Main clause \+ naru \+ cause clause**

*Î dîqab naru tye sha-turam.*  
“I am afraid because the animal is big.”

### **Cause clause first**

**Naru \+ cause clause, main clause**

*Naru tye sha-turam, î dîqab.*  
“Because the animal is big, I am afraid.”

Placing the cause first makes it the context for what follows. Placing it second presents it as an explanation of the main statement.

---

## **Result: narudi**

**Narudi** introduces a result, conclusion, or consequence. It corresponds to “therefore,” “so,” or “as a result.”

**Cause or premise clause \+ narudi \+ result clause**

*Tye sha-turam; narudi î dîqab.*  
“The animal is big; therefore, I am afraid.”

**Naru** and **narudi** express the same logical relationship from opposite directions:

**A naru B**  
“A happens because B.”

**B; narudi A**  
“B is true; therefore A happens.”

**Narudi** developed historically from **naru** and **udil**, but is treated as a separate word in modern Huntspeak.

---

## **Contrast: ara**

**Ara** joins two contrasting statements of equal grammatical importance.

**Clause \+ ara \+ clause**

*Î dîqab ara î rîsaš.*  
“I am afraid, but I fight.”

Neither clause is grammatically subordinate. Both are presented as independent assertions.

When the subject remains the same, it may be omitted from the second predicate:

*Î dîqab ara rîsaš.*  
“I am afraid but fight.”

---

## **Concession: qesha**

**Qesha** means “although,” “even though,” or “despite the fact that.”

It introduces a circumstance that might normally be expected to prevent the main event.

### **Concessive clause first**

**Qesha \+ concessive clause, main clause**

*Qesha î dîqab, î rîsaš.*  
“Although I am afraid, I fight.”

### **Main clause first**

**Main clause \+ qesha \+ concessive clause**

*Î rîsaš qesha î dîqab.*  
“I fight although I am afraid.”

### **Difference between ara and qesha**

**Ara** coordinates two equal statements:

*Î dîqab ara î rîsaš.*  
“I am afraid, but I fight.”

**Qesha** makes the fear background information:

*Qesha î dîqab, î rîsaš.*  
“Although I am afraid, I fight.”

The main assertion in the second sentence is that the speaker fights.

---

## **Irrealis: iqe**

**Iqe** is an irrealis particle. It marks an event as imagined, possible, conditional, proposed, or otherwise not established as real.

It is not a tense. The verb following **iqe** retains its ordinary tense and aspect marking.

**Iqe** normally appears immediately before the predicate it modifies.

**Subject \+ iqe \+ predicate**

Depending on context, the construction may mean:

* might do;  
* would do;  
* could possibly do;  
* imagine that someone does;  
* if someone does.

### **Conditional clauses**

When an irrealis clause is linked to a result clause, it functions as a condition.

**Iqe \+ condition clause, result clause**

*Iqe shiä sha-led, tye nenad.*  
“If the moon is bright, the animal moves.”

The conditional clause normally comes first because it establishes the circumstances under which the result applies.

It may follow the main clause when the result is emphasized:

*Tye nenad iqe shiä sha-led.*  
“The animal moves if the moon is bright.”

The precise difference between possible and counterfactual conditions is determined by tense, context, and any later mood rules.

---

## **Negative Irrealis: naaq-iqe**

**Naaq-iqe** marks a negative condition. It means “if not” or “unless.”

**Naaq-iqe \+ condition clause, result clause**

*Naaq-iqe shiä sha-led, tî mîda.*  
“Unless the moon is bright, we sleep.”

The literal logical meaning is:

> If the moon is not bright, we sleep.

**Naaq-iqe** is treated as a fixed compound particle and is written with a hyphen.

---

## **Purpose: wey**

**Wey** introduces the intended purpose, goal, or desired outcome of an action. It corresponds to “so that” or “in order that.”

**Main clause \+ wey \+ purpose clause**

*Î nîtar qäru wey radyävu leba qäru.*  
“I cook food so that the child can eat.”

The purpose clause normally follows the action whose purpose it explains.

### **Difference between cause and purpose**

*Naru* explains the reason an action occurs:

*Î nîtar qäru naru radyävu sha-kik’et.*  
“I cook food because the child is hungry.”

*Wey* explains what the action is intended to accomplish:

*Î nîtar qäru wey radyävu leba qäru.*  
“I cook food so that the child eats.”

A purpose is intended and may not yet have been achieved.

---

## **Simultaneous Time: eshu**

**Eshu** means “while” or “during the time that.”

It introduces an event occurring at the same time as the main event.

### **Temporal clause first**

**Eshu \+ temporal clause, main clause**

*Eshu se meda, î vîdab yasyi.*  
“While she sleeps, I write a tale.”

### **Main clause first**

**Main clause \+ eshu \+ temporal clause**

*Î vîdab yasyi eshu se meda.*  
“I write a tale while she sleeps.”

Both orders are grammatical. Clause-first order establishes the time frame; clause-second order adds the time frame after the main statement.

---

## **Later Time: avikh**

**Avikh** means “after.”

It introduces an event that occurs before the event in the main clause.

### **Temporal clause first**

**Avikh \+ earlier clause, later clause**

*Avikh tî rîsaš, tî lîba qäru.*  
“After we fight, we eat food.”

### **Main clause first**

**Later clause \+ avikh \+ earlier clause**

*Tî lîba qäru avikh tî rîsaš.*  
“We eat food after we fight.”

---

## **Earlier Time: tarem**

**Tarem** means “before,” “prior to,” or “earlier than.”

It is a dedicated temporal word and is distinct from spatial **dakhan**, “in front of.”

### **Temporal clause first**

**Tarem \+ later clause, earlier clause**

*Tarem tî mîda, tî bîsha.*  
“Before we sleep, we pray.”

### **Main clause first**

**Earlier clause \+ tarem \+ later clause**

*Tî bîsha tarem tî mîda.*  
“We pray before we sleep.”

---

## **Explicit Sequence: udil**

**Udil** means “then,” “next,” or “after that.”

It introduces the next event in a sequence.

**Clause \+ udil \+ clause**

*Î vîsat tye, udil î rîsaš.*  
“I see the animal, then I fight.”

### **Difference between event chaining and udil**

Event chaining leaves the sequence implicit:

*Î vîsat tye, rîsaš.*  
“I see the animal and fight.”

**Udil** makes the chronological progression explicit:

*Î vîsat tye, udil rîsaš.*  
“I see the animal, then fight.”

### **Difference between udil and narudi**

**Udil** expresses chronological sequence:

> This happened, then that happened.

**Narudi** expresses logical consequence:

> This is true; therefore, that follows.

Two events may occur in sequence without one causing the other. In that case, **udil** is used rather than **narudi**.
